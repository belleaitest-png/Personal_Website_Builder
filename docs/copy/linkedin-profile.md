## 2. About section (2,600 character limit)

```
I build applied AI products for health and food systems. Three are live, two more are in development.

The useful thing about my background is not that it is unusual. It is that both halves of it train the same muscle. Chartered accountancy is several years of learning to find the one number that does not reconcile and then explain why it matters. Harvard is two years of learning to ask whether the thing is worth doing at all. Put those together and you have most of the job when you are deciding what to build, which is the part nobody can hand to a model.

What I have built so far:

Apex Health OS reads WHOOP recovery, HRV, strain and sleep data, correlates it against what you actually eat, and generates the grocery list that follows from it. Built with Claude Code.

Bloom is a trimester-aware pregnancy health platform covering supplement guidance and nutrient gap detection. Built end to end in Lovable, down to the copy.

Demographic Forecaster maps country-level fertility decline and projects population trajectories. It started as a chart I needed for a paper at HBS. It became the thing that made the argument.

Two more are in development: an agent that keeps my professional relationships warm, and a supplement manager that catches ingredient overlap across brands.

I have tried around thirty AI tools and kept four. That ratio matters more than the number. Picking things up quickly stopped being a skill the moment everyone could do it. What is harder is putting things down, and knowing quickly that something is beautifully made and still wrong for the work.

The domain is food, fertility and metabolic health. That is where I have done the research, and where the data is richest and least used. My HBS work on declining birth rates and food systems kept arriving at the same place: the evidence already exists, spread across subsidy data and processing incentives and nutrition panels that nobody reads together. That stopped being a research problem some time ago. It is now a question of who has the judgement to connect it, and the patience to make the result worth looking at.

Two things I believe. The companies that matter next will sit where biology, food and AI meet, because biology now produces more data in a morning than anyone can read in a year. And when production gets cheap, the premium moves to taste: which problem, which words, what to leave out.

Looking for applied AI work where the hard part is the judgement, not the demo.

annabellebody.com
```
